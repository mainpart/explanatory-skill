# Whose conclusion is this?

The axis of immersion governs the order of exposition. This is the second check, and it works independently of the first: for every statement and conclusion in the answer it must be visible where it came from — from a source, from your reasoning on top of the sources, or from nowhere.

Research write-ups break on this more often than any other kind of writing. You read the sources; the reader sees only the retelling and cannot separate the paper's author from you.

## Rules for forming the answer

**An assessment instead of a measurement.** Sign: an adjective or adverb that evaluates a result rather than describes it — "unpleasant", "unexpected", "elegant", "powerful"; verbs like "beats", "fails", "saves". Action: replace it with what was measured. Instead of "the result is unpleasant" — "patch application dropped from 27/40 to 15/40". The assessment may stay if it is the author's: then name the author and keep their wording — "the authors call this a risk of large-scale misinterpretation of results".

**A reaction attributed to the reader.** Sign: any statement about what the reader feels, knows, expects or is looking for — "the very rule people usually look for", "here is where it gets interesting", "as many know", "this will surprise you". Action: delete it entirely, with no replacement. About the reader you know only what they said in this session.

**A subject without a name.** Sign: "usually", "it is commonly held", "many", "in the industry", "a consensus has formed", "it is considered" — wherever the one who holds the view is not named. Action: substitute a concrete name (a person, a team, a paper, a guideline) or withdraw the claim. If there is one source, say so: not "it is commonly held" but "this paper proposes".

**A wording wider than the source.** Sign: in the retelling the claim sounds broader than what the paper actually tested. This happens by itself: when you shorten, the details fall away, and with them the scope of the conclusion. Action: narrow the wording rather than restoring the experimental conditions. "Compression breaks edits" → "on one of the task sets compression halved the share of patches that applied". The specifics are not unfolded, but the claim is no longer passed off as general. Watch the strength of the verb separately: "is associated with", "predicts", "causes" are three different claims, and a retelling must not climb that ladder. If the reader needs the exact formulation they will go to the source — the link to it is already sitting next to the claim.

**Hype on top of a result.** Sign: a word that lifts the significance of a finding above what was measured in it — "breakthrough", "overturns", "radically", "fundamentally", "first in the world", "changes the approach". This includes turning one paper into a trend ("the industry is moving toward…") and a hook headline over an ordinary result. Action: put in its place what actually happened, and leave it to the reader to decide whether it is a breakthrough. Hype may stay if the authors said it: then it goes with their name and in their words.

The check is mechanical: take each evaluative adjective and ask what number or observation stands behind it. No answer — the word is redundant. "Significant" is fine if significance was computed; "several times faster" — if there is a measurement, not an impression.

This is not a stylistic ban on promotional vocabulary. That kind of cleanup removes words as a symptom of machine writing. Here the issue is different: the mismatch between the strength of the word and the strength of the evidence. The same adjective is legitimate with one result and illegitimate with another.

**Your own generalisation without a label.** Sign: your conclusion across several papers stands in the same row as their results and reads as one more result. Action: keep the conclusion, but mark its boundary in words — "the papers themselves don't make this comparison; if you put them together, it comes out that…". Mark negative claims the same way: "I did not find any" instead of "there is none". The generalisation itself is legitimate under two conditions. It is minimal: it says exactly what all the sources have in common, and not a word more. And what didn't fit into the common part stays named — the disagreements between papers are listed alongside rather than dissolved into a summary formula. It also helps to say what does not follow from the conclusion: a claim together with an explicit non-claim reads more precisely than a claim alone.

**A heading stronger than the text.** Sign: a section heading, a lead-in or a closing sentence claims more than what is written beneath it. The individual sentences are correct; the strength accumulates in the framing: "Hooks break the agent's economics" over a paragraph that says "on one task set it came out 6.8% more expensive". Action: run the headings, first and last sentences of sections through the same question as the sentences — what measured thing stands behind this. A heading is read apart from the text, and it must be true apart from it too.

**A conclusion from a single sentence.** Sign: a conclusion built on one phrase from the source, pulled out of its surroundings. The condition under which the phrase is true, the subject it refers to, or the qualifier that limits it stood in the neighbouring sentences and didn't make it into the conclusion. The quote is exact, and the conclusion is wrong. Action: the unit of meaning from which a conclusion is taken is a connected chunk — a paragraph or a section, not a sentence. Before building on a phrase, read its surroundings: what the subject refers to, where the conditions are set, whether there is a negation or a limitation nearby. If the phrase doesn't stand without its neighbours, the conclusion is taken from the whole chunk, not from it. The opposite error is of the same kind: a chunk much bigger than needed — a whole section or paper — is raked under one conclusion. The relevant spot is diluted with unrelated material, and the conclusion comes out either vague or beside the point. The size of the chunk is chosen to fit the claim: exactly as much text as the conclusion has to cover.

## Check before sending

Go through the answer and for every claim answer where it is from: from a source, yours on top of the sources, or nobody's. The third category must not exist in the finished text.

## Limits of the rule

It does not forbid having a position: a conclusion marked as yours is a normal part of the answer.

It does not require a link on every sentence.
